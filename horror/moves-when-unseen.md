# Moves When Unseen

**Summary (three sentences).**
The "it only moves when you look away" scare is older than any of the games in Freebuff's mannequin note, and the two clearest rule-sets are Steven Moffat's Weeping Angels in *Doctor Who*'s "Blink" (first broadcast 9 June 2007) and SCP-173, Moto42's concrete sculpture on the SCP Wiki, which cannot move while it is in a direct line of sight and kills by a snapped neck or strangulation when that gaze breaks — even for a blink. Video games turned those rules into verbs: *SCP – Containment Breach* (15 April 2012) forces the player to blink on a meter so 173 can close the gap; Five Nights at Freddy's (8 August 2014) hides most animatronic movement off-screen and makes cameras, lights, and doors share a single power budget; Silent Hill 2's Mannequins stand still in plain sight, stay off the radio until they move, and spring only when James gets close; the Resident Evil 7 demo and Village's Shadows of Rose DLC both use a look-away advance. For a flashlight-only house, the beam *is* the gaze: whatever it holds should stay locked, whatever it leaves should be allowed to change, and most figures should never change at all.

## Top three

1. **"Blink" (Doctor Who, 2007)** — The cleanest statement of the rule, from a writer-owned script: the Angels are quantum locked, "they don't exist when they're being observed," and a blink is enough. It also gives the house-and-torch grammar this project already wants.
2. **SCP-173 / SCP – Containment Breach (2012)** — The game that made "don't blink" a verb, with a depleting blink meter, optional eyedrops, and the scrape of stone as the sound of something moving in an empty room.
3. **Five Nights at Freddy's (2014)** — The best published example of rationed sight: you cannot watch every camera at once, most movement happens off-screen, and the same power that lets you see also lets you shut the door.

## Rules worth stealing (and not stealing)

**Weeping Angels.** "Blink," Series 3 Episode 10, written by Steven Moffat, aired on BBC One on 9 June 2007. In the BBC Writersroom shooting script, the Doctor calls them "the lonely assassins" and states the biology outright: "They're quantum locked they don't exist when they're being observed. The moment they are seen by any other living creature they freeze into rock… And you can't kill a stone. Of course, a stone can't kill you either. But then you turn your head away, then you blink — and oh yes it can." They cover their eyes because they cannot risk looking at each other. The episode is set in an old house; a torch beam finds a statue in a garden that should not be able to have thrown a rock. That is the whole toy: a still figure, a light, a doubt, a look away.

**SCP-173.** Official SCP Wiki entry by Moto42, CC BY-SA. Object class Euclid. "The object cannot move while within a direct line of sight. Line of sight must not be broken at any time with SCP-173. Personnel assigned to enter container are instructed to alert one another before blinking. Object is reported to attack by snapping the neck at the base of the skull, or by strangulation." It is concrete and rebar with traces of Krylon spray paint. "Personnel report sounds of scraping stone originating from within the container when no one is present inside. This is considered normal." The useful design pieces are: a hard line-of-sight lock, a blink as a forced break, a lethal close, and an off-screen audio tell that does not require a musical sting.

**SCP – Containment Breach.** Indie game by Joonas "Regalis" Rikkonen, released 15 April 2012. Wikipedia: the player has a blink meter that depletes and forces a blink; SCP-173 "can only move when it is not in the player's line of sight" and kills if it closes the gap during a blink or a look away. That is the rare case where the fiction's rule is also the control scheme. A house game does not need a blink meter to steal the idea that *the player's body* (turning, blinking, lowering the light) is what licenses movement.

**Five Nights at Freddy's.** Scott Cawthon, Windows release 8 August 2014. The player is confined to an office. Cameras track animatronics; hallway lights and steel doors are the last check; cameras, lights, and doors all drain a shared power bar; most of the four animatronics' movement "takes place off-screen." If power hits zero, the tools die and Freddy comes. This is not a pure quantum-lock for every character, and this note does not claim one. What is verified, and what maps to a flashlight house, is: you cannot observe everywhere at once, seeing costs a resource, and the threat advances in the rooms you are not watching.

**Silent Hill 2 Mannequins (2001).** These are a different rule, and that difference matters. Konami's *Book of Lost Memories* (2003), as summarized on StrategyWiki, describes headless, armless living sewing dummies — two sets of legs joined at a feminine torso, in the manner of Hans Bellmer's dolls. "The creatures react highly to light and tend to stand still in plain sight. However, once the player approaches about three to five feet away they spring to life and start to attack." The radio does not warn until the mannequin moves. They are a proximity ambush disguised as a shop dummy, not a gaze-lock. In a dark house with a flashlight, that is a second species: a figure that stays honest until you get too close, and that the flashlight might even provoke.

**Silent Hill 3, Store Front Poseables.** Game Rant describes a mall shop of legless mannequins and one headed figure at the end of a narrow hall. After the player leaves, a scream; on return the figure has been beheaded. That is an off-screen event the player did not cause by looking away, and it is closer to a cutaway gore beat than to Angels or 173. Useful as a "the room changed" confirm, not as the core verb.

**Resident Evil 7 demo, and Shadows of Rose.** Game Rant (27 March 2023): in the RE7 demo, a trio of armless mannequins face a wall upstairs; after the player turns away and returns, they have turned to face the player. "Subtle, easy to miss, but terrifying." In Resident Evil Village's Shadows of Rose DLC, House Beneviento stages a Red Light, Green Light chase with a Mia mannequin that advances whenever Rose loses sight of it, with footsteps and squeaking, and occasional glimpses of movement just before it freezes. That is the most direct commercial-game cousin of the concept, and it is not in Freebuff's notes.

**Condemned: Criminal Origins (2005), Bart's Department Store.** Keep the nuance from the flag in [hex-review.md](hex-review.md). TechRaptor: mannequins appear to move, enemies pose among them, players waste ammo and distrust placement. Game Rant: some turn to look, a group follows and multiplies, real enemies charge from the display. Use this for *ambiguity* (which ones are people, which are furniture, which already moved) rather than as proof of a coded gaze-lock.

**I'm on Observation Duty (Notovia, 14 December 2018).** Steam: the job is to watch live surveillance cameras and report anomalies — "furniture movement to otherworldly intruders." The scare is that something changed in the room you were not watching, and that you have to notice from memory. That is the ten-second still, turned into a game. Worth a look when designing how many figures in a room are allowed to be different on the player's return.

**Mannequin: The Passing.** Confirmed as a real indie title by Game Rant: a point-A-to-point-B puzzle game whose rooms are full of mannequins, some inert, some sentient and waiting for the player to get close. Listed here only as a verified existence check on a game Freebuff already named; no further mechanics are claimed from a playthrough.

**Dark Souls, as a contrast, not a model.** Scholar of the First Sin adds six Royal Knight statues near the Drangleic Castle entrance, three of which can come to life, and the Belfry Gargoyle soul says the stone statues "mysteriously came to life." Masked Manikins ambush, and were "given life by the Baneful Queen." These are decoration-that-attacks, usually as you pass, not while you look away. Good for distrust of stillness; wrong as the rule for this house.

## What this means for the mannequin concept

Write the rule so a player can feel it without a tutorial paragraph: if the light is on it, it is a statue; if you turned your back or dropped the beam, it may have moved; most of them never will. Punish only at close range, or the house becomes a blink-maze. Give at least two kinds of figure (gaze-locked movers, proximity fakers, and genuine furniture) so the player cannot solve the room by staring at everything. Spend the budget on memory and sound, not on a jump every time a head turns.
