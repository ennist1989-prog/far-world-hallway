# Occult and Folk-Horror Visual Language

**Summary (three sentences).**
Occult and folk-horror imagery works by making belief physical: candlelight instead of electric light, sigils and apotropaic marks scratched into doorframes and thresholds, veils and net curtains that half-hide a room, and handmade objects — stitched cloth, carved wood, wax, twine, human hair — whose visible labour implies someone who cared too much. The wrongness is usually a craft error rather than a monster: a face stitched slightly off-centre, a charm assembled from the wrong materials, a ritual performed in a kitchen instead of a church, so the uncanny arrives as something made by hand and slightly misremembered. Liminal domestic spaces carry it best — hallways, stairwells, utility rooms, back gardens at dusk — because the fear needs a place that is almost home but has stopped behaving like one.

## Top three

1. **The Wicker Man (1973)** — The template for folk horror's daylight dread, where ritual objects, masked processions and cheerful villagers make the intrusion of belief feel normal and therefore inescapable.
2. **Midsommar (2019)** — Pushes the same vocabulary into white linen, floral wreaths and painted runes, showing that folk horror can be beautiful, sunlit and still feel like a trap closing.
3. **Mundaun (2021)** — A game drawn in pencil on paper, where the handmade texture itself is the wrongness: charcoal shading, carved masks and Alpine ritual objects that look like they were made by someone in the village.
