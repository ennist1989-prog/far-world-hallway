# Aesthetic direction — one rule, two symptoms

**Summary (three sentences).**
The recommended direction is slow dread built on a single legible rule — attention licenses change — expressed twice: architecture that sags, warps or turns wet only when it has been out of sight, and mannequins that are still while watched and different in number when the player returns. The target feeling is not "haunted house" but **unreliable space**: a home that is quietly keeping track of where the player is looking, and figures that are furniture until they are not. This note is the short written summary the brief asked for; it does not replace the sourced notes, and every number in it is a proposal, not a measurement.

## 1. The intended feeling

Not fear of a monster. Fear of **having missed something in a room you thought you knew**. The player should leave a room confident, come back, and be unable to say what changed. The dominant emotions, in order:

1. **Doubt** — "were there three of them?"
2. **Paranoia** — "the house does this when I turn around."
3. **Isolation** — "no one else is here to confirm it."
4. **Recognition, late** — "it is closer, and I let it move."

Jump scares are not the product. The product is the ten seconds before the player decides to turn around.

## 2. One rule, two symptoms

The folder already contains the rule, in [lock-spec.md](lock-spec.md) (provisional): what the beam holds stays; what it leaves may change. The aesthetic consequence is that **walls and mannequins must obey the same physics of attention**, or the player will learn two rules and neither will feel supernatural.

- A wall that has been out of frame may be a few centimetres wrong.
- A figure that has been out of frame may be one metre closer, or one more in number.
- Neither changes while observed. Neither announces itself.

The player's reward for understanding the rule is not safety — it is the ability to **notice**. That is why the game must never explain it with a HUD counter or a tutorial voice.

## 3. Melting walls — what "wrong" should look like

Borrow the reference collection's structural reading, not the candle-wax cliché.

**Do**

- **Sag, lean, bladder.** A doorframe out of plumb by two or three centimetres. Wallpaper with a palm-sized blister. A skirting board that has bowed away from the wall. Plaster that has gone soft and slightly darker, as if damp.
- **One change per room per pass.** The player should be able to describe what changed in a sentence.
- **Local, not global.** The distortion belongs to a patch of wall, a threshold, a corner — never the whole room at once.
- **Keep the edges true.** Doorframes, picture rails and floor lines stay straight; only the surface between them fails. This is the Bacon lesson and the navigation guarantee.
- **Wetness as a material, not an effect.** A sheen that catches the flashlight, a normal map that reads as damp, a slow drip sound. Wet plaster is more wrong than running goo.

**Don't**

- Full-room Salvador Dalí melt. It reads as a funhouse, not a home.
- Distortion in the player's current view. If the player can watch it move, the rule is broken.
- Melting that blocks the only route. Unreadable navigation turns dread into annoyance.
- Colour-shifting slime. Keep the palette domestic: damp plaster, rust, tallow, dust.

**How far to push it:** at the subtle end, the player doubts their memory; at the overt end, they see a wall that has become a membrane. Use the subtle end for most of the game and allow **one** overt room, late, where the architecture is visibly organic. That room is the thesis; the rest is evidence.

## 4. Mannequins — stillness, then numbers

The figure work is not about movement. It is about **count and posture**.

- **Stillness is the default and must be convincing.** No idle sway, no breathing, no head tracking. If a figure fidgets while watched, the whole rule dies.
- **The count is the scare.** Three become four with the fourth between the player and the door. The added figure should be the one that matters — placed on the exit line, not in a corner.
- **Change posture, not pose-drama.** The RE7 trio simply turns to face the player. No lunge, no reach. The wrongness is that they have **noticed**.
- **Material:** warm off-white plastic, waxy pink, faintly jaundiced; seams and joints visible under the beam; clothing that is ordinary and slightly too clean or slightly too worn. Blank faces, or faces with one feature slightly off-centre. Avoid gore on the figures; the horror is that they are manufactured.
- **Density:** most rooms hold zero to two figures. A room that holds five is an event. Never fill a room to the point where the player stops counting.
- **Two species, per the research:** gaze-locked movers (the core rule) and proximity fakers that stay honest until close (the Silent Hill 2 model). A third category — genuine furniture — is what makes the other two legible.

## 5. Palette and material language

| Surface | Colour | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plaster / paint | bone, ash, damp grey-green | sags, blisters, darkens at the damp line |
| Woodwork | tar brown, exhausted varnish | bows, splinters, grain lifts |
| Metal | cold rust, dull brass | blooms, bleeds orange into adjoining paint |
| Fabric | faded floral, nicotine cream | hangs heavy, holds a body's imprint |
| Mannequin | dental cream, waxy pink, jaundice | catches the beam, never specular-white |
| Accent | oxide red **or** bile green, one per room | a single object, never a wash |

**Rule:** the palette is domestic and desaturated, with **one** saturated accent per room. When the room turns, the accent is what the eye finds first. Use that to place the clue or the added figure.

## 6. Light

- **The beam is the narrator.** It should carve a cone, not light a room; everything outside it is a legitimate blind spot. This is already argued in [hex-review.md](hex-review.md).
- **Practicals are islands.** A fridge glow, a hallway bulb, a television left on. They give the player somewhere to stand and something to switch off.
- **Darkness has a floor.** Keep ambient light just high enough to read doorframes and floor edges; pure black is for the moments the beam is off, not the baseline.
- **Flicker is a tool with a legal limit.** No more than three flashes per second, and no strobing during the fail state; [impact-frame-and-pacing.md](impact-frame-and-pacing.md) has the photosensitivity rules.

## 7. Sound pairing

- **Baseline:** room tone, refrigerator hum, a clock, pipe knock, the house settling. The sound of an ordinary home with no one in it.
- **Evidence of movement:** material sound, not music. A joint squeak, a plaster crack, a floorboard, fabric dragging. If a figure moved, the player should hear *what it is made of*.
- **No stingers on first sight.** The RE7 demo's attic reveal is silent; that is why it works.
- **One sound per change.** Layer two and the player reads it as a set piece.
- **Silence is a cue.** Pull the room tone for a beat before a change, then bring it back slightly wrong (a hum a semitone lower, a clock missing a tick).

## 8. How far to push it — the subtlety dial

| Tier | What happens | Frequency | Player reaction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Almost nothing | A chair has moved; a door is ajar; a figure's head is turned | Common | "Did I do that?" |
| 2. Structural | Wall sag, new damp patch, one extra figure, a doorway slightly narrower | Uncommon | "The house is doing this." |
| 3. Overt | A wall is visibly organic; a room's geometry has changed; the count doubles | Once or twice, late | "I understand what this place is." |

**Recommended mix:** roughly 70% tier 1, 25% tier 2, 5% tier 3. If tier 3 happens early, the rest of the game is spent waiting for a repeat instead of doubting.

**Readability floor:** the player must always be able to find a door, a stair and a light. Distortion may make a route *unpleasant* to look at, never *impossible* to read. If a playtester gets lost twice in the same room, the room is over-distorted.

## 9. What to avoid

- **Cartoon goo.** Dripping, bubbling, rainbow-slime walls. It moves the genre from dread to comedy.
- **Movement in view.** A figure that twitches while watched breaks the one rule the game has.
- **Count jumps in frame.** Adding a figure at the edge of the screen reads as a spawn, not a haunting.
- **Stinger stacking.** Music hits on every reveal teach the player to wait for the noise instead of watching the room.
- **A HUD.** No "figures: 3" counter, no sanity meter, no detection indicator. The player's memory is the instrument.
- **Explaining the house.** A lore document that says "the walls melt when unobserved" converts a rule into a mechanic.

## 10. Open questions

These are for Thomas, and several depend on the lock-spec decisions D1–D6.

1. **Is the melting local and authored, or systemic?** Authored patches are controllable and cheap; a systemic rule is more unsettling but risks the whole house looking ill.
2. **Do changes persist?** If a wall stays sagged, the house accumulates damage and the player can track it. If it resets, the house feels like it is dreaming. Recommended: persist within a run, reset on reload.
3. **Does the melting ever hurt?** A wall that can trap or crush introduces a second mechanic. Recommended: no; it disorients and reveals, never kills.
4. **Can distortion reveal routes?** A wall that thins enough to show a room beyond is a puzzle verb. It also doubles the level-design cost.
5. **What is the maximum figure count per room?** The aesthetic needs a number: proposed cap of five or six, with most rooms at zero to two.
6. **Is there a story cause?** The folder's story brief (next-research #10) is unwritten. The aesthetic works without an explanation, but the ending will need one — or a deliberate refusal.
7. **How much of the house is allowed to be organic at the end?** The single overt room needs a placement decision before any level work starts.
