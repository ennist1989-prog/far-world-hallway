# Condemned: Bart's Department Store — mannequin behaviour sheet

**Summary (three sentences).**
Chapter 5 of *Condemned: Criminal Origins* (2005) puts the player in an abandoned department store where the enemy faction dresses in the store's own white shirts and blue suits, so the living figures and the retail dummies are indistinguishable until one of them is struck or moves. The documented behaviour is ordinary ambush AI wearing a costume: the living mannequins stand motionless among the displays, begin to walk — often to pick up a weapon — when the player is not looking at them, and flank in small groups rather than freezing under a gaze like SCP-173. The one beat that reads as authored rather than emergent is the escalation tableau, where a row of figures is reported to reorder and multiply around the player between looks, and no source explains it in AI terms.

## Three kinds of figure in the room

| Figure | What it is | Does it move | What gates it | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retail dummy | Set dressing, identical costume to the enemies | No | Nothing — it is scenery | Weapon/enemy guides describe the living ones as blending among "the many department store dummies" |
| Living mannequin | Faction member (*The Influenced*) in a white shirt and blue suit, plastic-looking skin that cracks away when hit | Yes — a brisk walk, weapons picked up off the floor and displays | Being unobserved, plus ordinary combat AI; it hides and ambushes rather than charging | Joylock's weapon/enemy guide; Rely on Horror's account of one "sauntering" to a weapon |

## Camera gating — what Condemned actually does

- **Not a quantum lock.** Nothing in the sources describes a figure freezing mid-stride because it is being watched in the SCP-173 sense. The living mannequins are normal enemies whose opening move is to stay still; they are dangerous because you cannot tell still-and-inert from still-and-waiting.
- **Observation-gated movement, at least at the start.** Rely on Horror: "Depending on where the player looks, they might see a mannequin begin to move on its own. It will saunter over and pick up a nearby weapon before going into hiding to surprise the player." Uppercut describes the same first beat — a figure stepping off its stand, walking to a two-by-four, picking it up.
- **The movement is continuous and goal-directed.** This is the important structural difference from the RE7 demo and from Containment Breach: the player is not shown a new pose, they are shown a figure *doing something* — crossing a floor, collecting a weapon, choosing a hiding spot. If the camera catches it, the illusion is broken permanently, which is why the game leans on the player walking forward rather than turning.
- **No rule is ever stated.** No HUD, no tutorial line, no blinking meter. The player infers the rule from having been ambushed once.

## Group behaviour and the follow/multiply beat

- Three living mannequins are placed around the first ambush room. Rely on Horror's read: fix your attention on one and the others coordinate and come from behind.
- Game Rant describes five well-dressed figures in a row that are then noticed "closely following the player every step of the way; reappearing around every corner, multiplying each time."
- The same piece notes red herrings: some figures shift to watch from a distance while a genuine enemy charges in — the costume is used to misdirect attention as well as to hide.

## Tells the player can actually use

- **Noise is inverted here.** Other enemies in the game shout and announce themselves; mannequins are "uncharacteristically quiet", so silence is the tell rather than sound. This is the opposite of SCP-173's scraping and Shadows of Rose's footsteps.
- **Damage is the only reliable proof.** Plastic skin cracks and falls away when struck, exposing a skinless face, so a hit answers the question.
- **Striking a still one kills it instantly** if it is mid-mimicry — the game rewards guessing correctly.

## Scripted versus systemic

| Beat | Verdict | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mannequin walks to a two-by-four and picks it up | Systemic — emergent from enemy AI | Weapon pickup is a universal enemy behaviour in this game; the disguise is the only special part |
| Three figures flanking while the player faces one | Systemic, or at most lightly staged | Described as a consequence of where the player is looking, not a fixed animation |
| Mannequins with shotguns running | Scripted, explicitly | The weapon guide states mannequins "cannot run (except in 1 or 2 pre-scripted sequences involving Mannequins with shotguns)" |
| A row of figures reordered and multiplied between looks | Reads as scripted set piece; unresolved | Two write-ups describe it as unexplained, with no AI rule attached; it is the one moment where the game appears to cheat on the player's behalf |
| A figure shifting to watch from afar while a real enemy charges | Staged misdirection | Described as a red herring rather than a combat behaviour |

## What would settle the remaining questions

Nothing here came from playing the chapter; it is reconstructed from guides and two essays. To close it:

1. **Does a mannequin ever move while fully in frame?** Walk backwards facing one across the open floor. If it holds, movement is observation-gated; if it closes, it is plain ambush AI.
2. **Is the reorder beat deterministic?** Save before the row, reload twice. Identical repositioning means scripted; variation means a hidden system.
3. **How many are living, per room?** The guides give "3" for the first fight and "five" for the row, which may be counting different rooms.
4. **What is the actual speed?** The guides call it a "brisk walk" and say they never run; a stopwatch reading in body-lengths per second is what the reference sheet needs.

## What it argues for in Far World

- **Costume beats animation.** The cheapest version of this scare is a figure that is *already correct* for the room — same clothes, same pose as the furniture — so the player cannot audit it. The RE7 mannequin trio works the same way.
- **A continuous off-screen action is scarier than a pose swap.** A figure that moved a chair, took a tool, or shut a door tells the player something happened without showing it. That is a cheaper and more legible effect than teleporting a silhouette closer.
- **Silence is a usable tell, but only if the house is otherwise quiet.** If every room has a sound bed, the absence of footsteps stops meaning anything.
- **One authored escalation is worth more than three systemic ones.** The reorder tableau is remembered more than the flanking AI, precisely because it breaks the rule the player had learned.
