# Dark Souls: Statues, Mannequins and Environmental Storytelling

**Summary (three sentences).**
Dark Souls treats statues as furniture that might be a threat: stone knights, gargoyles and faceless figures stand in alcoves until you walk past, and Dark Souls II makes the idea literal with the Masked Manikins — headless puppets given life by the Baneful Queen — and the Belfry Gargoyles, statues whose own soul description says they "mysteriously came to life" the moment the bell wakes them. Because the games rarely distinguish decoration from enemy in advance, players learn to read silhouettes and to distrust stillness, which is the mannequin fear converted into a mechanic. The environmental storytelling does the rest: item placement that implies ownership, corpses posed mid-action, landmarks visible from a distance and later walkable, and item descriptions that let the world explain itself without a cutscene.

## Top three

1. **Dark Souls (2011)** — The foundational text, where a statue is scenery until it is not, and where the whole story is told through level geometry and item text rather than exposition.
2. **Dark Souls II: Scholar of the First Sin (2015)** — The most explicit version of the mannequin idea, adding ambushing Manikins and Royal Knight statues at Drangleic Castle that stand up when you pass.
3. **Dark Souls III (2016)** — Refines the ambush-as-architecture language, using cathedral statuary, corpse clusters and sightlines to imply a history the player arrives too late to witness.
